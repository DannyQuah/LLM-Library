Course title - AI and the New Asian State: Remaking Political Economy  

Combines in an inter-disciplinary way:
- Economics: three key concepts (1) Task substitution and complementarity; (2) Productivity, growth, and technological diffusion; (3) Comparative advantage and international economic interdependence.
- Political science - three key concepts (1) The developmental state and its historical arc; (2) Distributional conflict, legitimacy, and the social compact; (3) Sovereignty, technological dependence, and strategic interdependence.

By the end of the course, students will be able to
1. Explain the economic mechanisms by which AI can potentially transform Asia's economies;
2. Identify key institutional, political, and structural bottlenecks for how these mechanisms operate in Asia;
3. For specific Asian economies, propose feasible, evidence-based strategies for an overarching political economy capable of harnessing AI for growth and social development.

The course takes the form of a design studio that combines short lectures, regular student updates on current developments, individually-written policy memos on a specific AI-related policy challenge, and collaborative exercises to assemble a team-written final-project White Paper on AI strategy for a specific team-designated Asian economy.  Students will be assigned to country teams at the beginning of the course.  Then, over the duration of the course, country teams will maintain an AI-Readiness Political Economy Country Profile, for briefing course participants at regular intervals, thereby providing an up-to-date empirical dimension to the course.

Your task, based on our discussions and the course specification just given, is to 
1. Write a 100-word course description; a suggestion is that the summary can contain some appropriate version of "In this course, AI is a technological disruption that transforms production, markets, and economic growth. What state institutions and policy capabilities will Asia's societies need when the economic foundations for their development change?  To address this question the course examines the interaction between technological change, economic incentives, institutions, political power, and distributional outcomes."
2. Building on the outline you have already put together and that we have discussed, set down for each of the thirteen weekly 3-hour sessions of the semester a topic sequence and corresponding reading list:
 a. As you say, the critical question for inclusion of either topic or reading is "Does this material help students understand why AI might require a fundamentally different Asian state, and what that state should do?"
 b. For each session the readings should not be overly lengthy (30-40 pages at most, or less if the material is analytically demanding) but should come with three implicit layers: core theory; empirical confrontation; further exploration depending on student interest and skillset; build the reading list from the items in AI-Economics-IR/ but flag if you feel we should add appropriate external readings that you found in outside sources.  This last is not essential, only if you feel it important to add readings we have not yet discussed and placed in AI-Economics-IR/ .
 c. Each session has over its three hours students either provide an update on their Country Profile or brief on a particularly striking development that week in AI and Asia (30 mins); a short lecture by me (45 mins); and then student/team collaborative exercises that build towards the White Paper.
3. Write the assignment for individually-written 1200-word policy memos due in Week 6 that surfaces the urgency on a specific AI-related policy challenge (for that student's designated team country).
4. Write the assignment for team-written 6000-word White Paper due in Week 13 that provides an AI strategy for the team's designated country. Emphasise how the White Paper is about assembling the work that students have done in the weekly meetings over the semester.

Finally, write a rationale for the course addressing (a) the urgency and policy relevance of what the course covers; (b) its fit with a Master's degree at schools of public policy worldwide but at LKYSPP in particular; (c) its social science coverage, rather than that of technology, engineering, law, or computing; (d) its focus on Asia's development and developmental state, rather than broadly "AI and economics and political science".
