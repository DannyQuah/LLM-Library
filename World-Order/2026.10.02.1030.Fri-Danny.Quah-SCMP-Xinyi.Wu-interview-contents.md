**G-minus Southeast Asia**  
// The G-minus idea applied to Southeast Asia - summarize current thinking  

1. G-minus is the collection of institutions and norms that remain functional even if Great Powers are absent, unwilling, or obstructive.  G-minus is decidedly not about excluding the Great Powers.
2. How to think about this?  Leadership bundles together operations. Leadership is something we all look to because it is more efficient to run operations when they are thus bundled together.  For eight decades after the Second World War, the US provided multilateral rules and supported open economic exchange.  The elements of that system included market access, security, enforcement, crisis insurance, treaty formation, and international coordination. Benevolent leadership over the latter half of the 20th century assumed responsibility for these.  Put differently, the benevolent hegemon provided critical global public goods.
3. G-minus is not about looking for or replacing leadership but asking what functions can be unbundled but still have their beneficial effects available through decentralised arrangements.  These will necessarily not have the same level of efficiency as under benevolent leadership. But that's no reason to rule out a G-minus arrangement, especially if benevolent leadership is unavailable.  We can think of these --- G-minus, benevolent hegemony --- as different locations on a sliding scale of international order.  G-minus shifts attention away from who has the overwhelming power to maintain order to who has the incentive to support order at the level asked for by whatever location in the sliding scale of international order we can achieve.  It reduces the elasticity of international order with respect to Great Power engagement.
3. Southeast Asia has disproportionate incentive to have some such arrangement of international order.  SEA has benefited from the predictability and level-playing field provisions of rules-based multilateralism, using that system's provision of cross-border trade, investment, and production networks to overcome the disadvantages of small domestic markets.  But the world is moving away from that international order towards, instead, geoeconomic actions, strategic and sometimes weaponised interdependence, and power-asymmetric unilateralism.  This will have outsized negative effects on Southeast Asia.
4. Small states in SEA cannot simply self-insure or become autarkic.  And they certainly cannot consider projecting coercive power.  The further mistake would be if they waited for the US to return to its historical role of benevolent hegemony, or for China to step up and provide those global public goods, or for Japan/Australia/ or other middle powers to replace either of those.
5. ASEAN and ASEAN Centrality don't put up candidacy for overt regional or world leadership.  Instead, they provide demonstration possibilities for how functionality is possible even without Great Power presence or permission.  They seek to surface those problem domains where power becomes less necessary.  ASEAN has spent sixty years investigating the different dimensions where sovereign states can make cooperation routine when none has power enough to command the others.


**Southeast Asia between China and the US**

// SEA sees its patterns of trade, investment, and financial transactions evolving from rules-based multilateralism to a system of geoeconomic actions and strategic interdependence. The principal drivers used to be economic efficiency and comparative advantage.  Now the international system that SEA has to navigate is one where Beijing seeks economic and political influence, international expansion, and diversification, and Washington focuses on geostrategic competition with Beijing.  Implicitly keeping an eye on the overarching G-minus narrative, how is SEA re-examining simultaneously, on the one hand, its supply chains, Chinese ties, and Beijing's moves on yuan internationalisation  and, on the other hand, Trump's tariffs, transshipments scrutiny, and US economic statecraft more generally?  More generally, is SEA moving towards choosing a side in this exercise of re-examination?

1. My own guess is that there's no simple choosing sides outcome - although there have certainly been a number of high-profile studies engaging on that (the ISEAS annual surveys with a forced, artificial choice, or even more sophisticated ones asking about positioning in the space spanned by the Great Powers, i.e., generalized hedging).
2. What emerging evidence suggests instead is SEA reorganising its economic relationships so that it can continue to transact with both Beijing and Washington while reducing its vulnerability and dependency on either one.
3. In 2024 the US was ASEAN's largest external export destination, taking roughly 16% of ASEAN merchandise exports; China took 14.9%. But on the import side China supplied fully 25.5% of ASEAN imports. By 2025 ASEAN-China merchandise trade had reached $913bn; China remained ASEAN's largest trading partner and was its third-largest FDI source, with $26.2bn of investment.
3. "China + 1" is happening but not "China plus one minus China".  That is, China is not being taken out of the equation.  SEA is not doing China-decoupling. China is deepening its economic relationships with SEA while at the same time SEA is trying to increase its relative footprint in global supply chains that involve China.
4. Trump administration scrutiny is turning out to geopolitically pressure "rules of origin".  ASEAN's response is to deepen intra-ASEAN economic integration.  The upgraded ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement now highlights ASEAN cumulation, so originating inputs from other ASEAN members count toward the origin of final goods.  The arms race that is happening is whether ASEAN can do that sufficiently to overtake the Trump administration charge of being simply a portal of transshipment.  This flags how what Washington calls transshipment overlap uncomfortably with what Southeast Asia legitimately considers to be simply participation in increasingly elaborate regional value chains.
5. China is more than just a manufacturing powerhouse. It is simultaneously a gigantic market, supplier, investor, production ecosystem, and provider of financial infrastructure.  By end-2024, 150 ASEAN financial institutions were participating in China's CIPS (Cross Border Interbank Payment System) system.  China-ASEAN CIPS transactions reached RMB21.2 trillion, reportedly almost double the previous year's level.  SEA's participation has increased in clearance and settlements systems that avoid SWIFT - projects mBridge and Nexus were incubated at BIS and are now in operation involving a number of high-profile Southeast Asian economies.
6. Alternative pipes, not replacement reserve currencies.
7. Not about choosing sides but about whether SEA is successfully expanding optionalities or seeing them shut down.
