In G-minus discussion, cooperation is an important alternative conceptual counterweight to leadership: if we have cooperation, do we need leadership? Closely related, can cooperative behaviour ("inadvertent cooperation") emerge without leadership? (These are rhetorical for now, don't attempt to answer them! I also don't intend to suggest cooperation and leadership are alternatives, i.e., that the world is one of cooperation vs leadership. Instead, the questions as stated are intended to point to distinguishing cooperation that depends on leadership versus cooperation that does not.  My more focused questions to follow concern John Ikenberry's formulation of international order and how that can help us analyse international cooperation.)

I want to put down an agreed-on context for the discussion. For me, that begins with Hobbes and Locke. Let me put down my quick overview for you to refine.  

Hobbes's famous quote (from Leviathan Ch. 13) is almost a direct statement that refutes the possibility of cooperation without leadership:

"... during the time men live without a common power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called war; and such a war as is of every man against every man. (...)  In such condition there is no place for industry... no arts; no letters; no society; and which is worst of all, continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."  

For Hobbes, the only way out is that humanity surrenders to Leviathan---an absolute, undivided sovereign power---with unrelenting authority over them.  This is needed because humanity cannot be trusted to operate without such a sovereign.  The contract is not a limited agreement between individuals and the state but instead near-total surrender.  Humanity is intelligent enough to see mutual advantage, to come to agreements, and to understand laws of nature. Humanity has the capacity to value peace: that is why they establish a commonwealth.  But they also know that others' compliance remain uncertain, so they rationally fear betrayal. This calculation undermines agreement and trust.  For Hobbes, therefore, **cooperation is fundamentally precarious unless an effective and reliable enforcement mechanism is available**.  

John Locke reckons the state of nature is not as Hobbes describes. Instead one problem he seeks to solve is how to limit political authority given that individuals are already bound by a law of reason even before any government exists. For Locke, political authority is justified only to the extent it preserves the pre-existing natural rights implied by his (Lockean, distinctly non-Hobbesian) assumption of the state of nature.  Provided government does this, in Locke's view, individuals willingly hand over power to that centralised body of authority.  In the American Declaration of Independence that came nearly a century after, this is articulated as "the consent of the governed".  Individuals find it advantageous to surrender power to an authority because decentralised enforcement is otherwise deficient and incomplete, while at the same time they retain certain inalienable rights---resistance and replacement---over that centralised political authority.  

Therefore, what Locke sets out to resolve is indeed the challenge of bringing out cooperation among individuals.  He does so, however, through a centralised political authority that has its boundaries defined by a tradeoff: On the one hand, that authority is itself constrained because it needs to preserve the natural rights of individuals; on the other hand, that central authority needs to be sufficiently powerful to serve the needs of individuals in ways they could not themselves provide.

Ikenberry gets from Locke both sides of that tradeoff but necessarily transformed. The original Lockean formulation had, on the one hand,  restriction on the boundaries of political authority and, on the other, the willingness of individuals to cooperate with that authority by agreeing to form a government.  In Ikenberry's conceptualisation of the liberal international order, the second of these is clear: "free autonomous liberal democratic states rationally decide to transfer power to a liberal Leviathan" much as Locke might have supposed free rational individuals agree to constituting a civil government.  

The first of these takes several more steps in the reasoning.

For Locke individuals handing over power to political authority comes with that authority preserving the pre-existing natural rights of the individual.  This is the "consent of the governed" clause of the US Declaration of Independence.  For Locke, the people retain ultimate authority over their government.  

For Ikenberry and international society it is unclear how sovereign nations can have similar rights over any central authority of global governance. In Ikenberry's rendering, postwar liberal order rests on a bargain related to but different from Locke: **other states accept American leadership because America agrees to embed its power in rules and institutions that limit the arbitrary exercise of that power.**  This is importantly different from Locke. The international order did not offer a government whose authority derived from the collective consent of sovereign equals in the same way that Lockean government derived from the people. American leadership in world order remained grounded in a profound asymmetry of material power. Institutions constitutionalised aspects of that asymmetry; they did not eliminate it.

This is trickier than its Lockean counterpart.  But just as how individuals in Lockean society draw benefit from centralised political authority, so too states in the international community see returns to participation: institutions provide weaker states with access, voice, security, economic opportunity, and some capacity to influence the terms of their relationships with the dominant power.

At the same time, Ikenberry acknowledges the Hobbesian imperative, in a way that Locke did not:

"[P]ower and rules are not enemies; they can be friends, and they are both necessary in the production of liberal order(...). [A]ppeal to laws and what is right is useless without the ability to back it up with force (...). The United States has been one of the most successful order-building states in world history because it has combined the exercise of its power with the championing of rule-based order."

Locke supplies Ikenberry's constitutional logic: political authority becomes acceptable when it is exercised through rules that protect the autonomy and interests of those subject to it. Hobbes supplies the mechanism of assurance: rules cannot sustain cooperation if actors lack confidence that commitments will be honoured. In the liberal international order, however, the power-based enforcement mechanism is not central the way it is in Hobbesian society.  For Ikenberry, power does matter but it is that power that becomes more effective and more acceptable when exercised through institutions that also constrain its possessor.  

It seems to me critical questions come in two inter-related sets. First, under the US-dominated liberal international order, what was the goal of US leadership, and what was the nature of cooperation among the other, weaker states? Second, what possibilities for cooperation remain when that order has its leadership no longer keeping to its traditional role in the system?
