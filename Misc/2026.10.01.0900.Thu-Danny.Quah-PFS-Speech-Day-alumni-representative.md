PFS Speech Day 2026 // Alumni Representative Speech  
by Danny Quah  
Thu 01 Oct 2026 0900h  

I am a relic.

But I'm not the only one. Everyone in my generation is a relic too.

I left the Penang Free School in 1976. That is 50 years ago: 50 cohorts of Frees, two non-overlapping generations of parents and children—perhaps one lifetime.

The world that today's Free inhabits would have been almost unimaginable to us.

You are growing up with the world's entire stock of knowledge permanently in your pocket.  You have search engines, social media, instantaneous worldwide communication---and now AI and large language models.

We had slide rules. We watched black-and-white television. We spoke to people---not pinged them---on coin-operated rotary phones.

Studying meant working through the past ten years of exam papers---and they really were _papers_: passed down by seniors, sometimes with handwritten notes and sample answers.

We listened to Reddifusion and watched RTM. We didn't stream K-dramas or watch TikTok videos of K-pop girl groups and boy bands.

Mine is the generation that was conscious both before and after the Internet. If humanity is lucky and digital technology continues to advance, mine might also be the only generation ever able to point to our having one foot in each side of that divide.  

And life before the Internet was different in ways that are difficult to explain to today's young.  

Mine was the generation that got lost a lot.

Getting around Penang by bicycle or bus---sometimes several buses---required planning, memory, and occasionally asking strangers for directions.

For entertainment, we caught spiders from hibiscus hedges in Ayer Itam, Jelutong, and Balik Pulau---or from bougainvillea bushes around PFS.  We kept these spiders in matchboxes and duelled them at break time.

When friends left Penang, we wrote letters by hand.  Sometimes, on especially thin paper, we sent aerogrammes.  Then we waited weeks for a reply.

We had penpals.  (Does anyone still know what a penpal is?)

When we walked out the front door in the morning, nobody knew where we were. There was no Find My Phone.  Nobody could message us and expect an instant reply.

We constantly waited---for the bus, for next week's television episode, for a library book to be returned so that we could have our turn.

Enormous stretches of our lives left absolutely no record.  No Instagram posts.  No Discord trail.  Most of our mistakes simply disappeared into memory.

The 2026 Free lives with **instant knowledge, continuous connection, digital identity, machine intelligence, and collapsed distance**.

The 1976 Free lived with **unreachability, waiting, uncertainty, shared scarcity, and forgetting**.

Your world is more comfortable. But it is also more complicated.

You face climate change and extreme weather. You have grown up much more conscious of mental health and neurodiversity. You confront geopolitical conflict and the possibility that AI will transform the jobs you hope to have—or, some fear, threaten something much larger than just employment and markets.

And there is another important difference.

When my generation was ignorant, it was usually because **information was scarce**.

You inhabit a world of deepfakes, influencers, algorithmic feeds, misinformation, and contradictory narratives. When you don't know something, it is often because **information is abundant**.

When we were 17, we worried mostly about what might happen to _us_.  You're 17, but your worries are systemic and planet-sized.

But here is the optimistic side.

The tools you have today to change that world are more powerful than anything we could have imagined in 1976.

You can access almost all human knowledge at practically no cost. You can learn from Nobel Prize winners, study quantum mechanics, learn to code, or master another language---anytime, from anywhere.

With a laptop, you can write an app, compose music, publish an essay, start a business, build an AI service.  You can reach a teacher, an entrepreneur, a policymaker, or a potential collaborator on the other side of the world.

If the people immediately around you don't share your strange enthusiasm---for mathematics, dance, philosophy, entrepreneurship, music, vibe coding, whatever—you can find your own community somewhere in the world.

That matters. Finding your own community can give you permission to re-imagine your life.  

So, yes, you shoulder now bigger problems than we did at your age. But you also inherit fare more powerful tools for doing something about them than we could possibly have imagined.

Fifty years ago, my generation walked out of this school into our world.

Now it is your turn.

**Go out and make your world a better world.**
