Milestone 1: Policy Memo: JACOB GEORGE

POLICY MEMORANDUM

 

To:                Her Excellency Khiev Sophany, Secretary of State (Economic Diplomacy), Ministry of Foreign Affairs and International Cooperation

From:            Jacob George, Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy, National University of Singapore

Date:             17th September 2026

Subject:         Exposure of Cambodian textile & garment sector to U.S duties from Chinese subsides

 

Summary

Since the onset of the first US-China trade war in 2018, ASEAN has increasingly benefited from supply chain diversification by firms pursuing the "China plus one" strategy. World Bank research finds that countries replacing China as suppliers to the United States are those most deeply embedded in Chinese supply chains. Cambodia is one of them, and it’s now under American scrutiny for Chinese ownership and transshipment, which creates a distinctive structural risk of asymmetric entrapment.

Cambodia views tariffs and transshipment as the main trade risk with U.S but a new trade remedy rule creates a different risk, as Chinese subsidies can now follow Chinese owned production into Cambodia. In April 2025, the US Commerce Department (DoC) made one of its first findings that Chinese state subsidies reaching Chinese-owned plants in a third country can be charged against the exporting country. The U.S. first exercised this power when countervailing rates of 3,403.96% and 534.67%, along with antidumping duties of 117.18%, were imposed on the Cambodian solar sector.

Our garment and textile sector has the similar ownership, inputs exposure to China and the same SEZ model but it accounts for 46.8% of merchandise exports and roughly 1.1 million jobs. This threat is a legal and administrative procedure within the U.S. and outside the ambit of traditional diplomacy.

Background

The U.S. trade remedy system uses either antidumping duties, which offset goods sold below fair value, or countervailing duties to offset foreign government subsidies. Until 2024, a DoC regulation, 19 C.F.R. § 351.527, barred it from counting subsidies paid by one government to producers located in another country. The DoC repealed it in March 2024 and published the Regulations Improving and Strengthening the Enforcement of Trade Remedies, 2024. Now, for the first time, duties can be imposed on Chinese-owned factory in Cambodia that receive support from the Chinese government.

The first test came within months. A coalition of U.S. solar manufacturers petitioned, and in April 2025, the DoC found that producers in Cambodia, Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam had received subsidies from the government of China. Under this regulation, the U.S. imposed countervailing duties of 3,403.96% on some producers and 534.67% on others, alongside antidumping duties reaching 117.18%. At such rates, access to the U.S. market becomes commercially unviable. The impact was essentially a prohibition on trade. Exports collapsed from USD 2.07 billion in 2023 to roughly USD 6 million in 2025.

Garments and textile sector has a similar structure, though the nature of Chinese subsidies remains unknown. The regulation is focused on those who owns factories and the support the owner receives from a third party State. Chinese investors supplied 73% of Cambodia's USD 5.2 billion in 2025 foreign direct investment, roughly 90% of garment factories are Chinese-owned; and 80% of fabric and 80% of yarn came from China. Solar was not as large a sector when it was destroyed. Garments, footwear and travel goods earned USD 7.99 billion in the first half of 2026 or 46.8% of merchandise exports and the U.S is the biggest export market.

Trade-remedy cases begin with private petitions from U.S. firms and run on statutory deadlines under administrative rule, solar petition was filed under the Biden administration. A likely petitioner in this case will be the National Council of Textile Organizations, who has argued for 30 years that Chinese subsidies cost American textile jobs and now has the instrument it once lacked. The consequences of such an action would be severe and largely outside diplomatic control. 

Should its members petition on textiles or garments, the sequence largely outside our control. DoC sends subsidy questionnaires to the Cambodian exporters, to our government, and to Beijing. Beijing doesn’t answer them, it didn’t in the solar case. Section 776 of the Tariff Act of 1930 then permits Commerce, where a party has failed to cooperate to the best of its ability, to draw an adverse inference and fill the gap from sources including the petition. In countervailing duty cases, it may apply a rate for the same or a similar programme from another proceeding involving the same country, and may choose the highest such rate.

Therefore, a decision taken in Beijing not to answer an American questionnaire becomes a duty paid by Cambodian factories and Cambodian workers. Nor can Cambodia retaliate. Since, the U.S. bought USD 15.3 billion of our goods in 2025 and sold us USD 370 million. This is what Chhay Lim calls asymmetric entrapment, as we depend on two rival networks, each able to close a chokepoint it controls, and what one hub demands, now reaches into the other's. Room to manoeuvre hasn’t collapse but it is eroding for Cambodia.

Options

Option 1: Continue through the diplomatic channel alone.

  • Pros: There is no fiscal cost and the bilateral channel is open, which has delivered on headline tariff rates.
  • Cons: Trade-remedy proceedings are not a purely diplomatic issues. Neither the subsequent trade agreement nor Cambodia's support on the ceasefire led to any relief on the solar orders, which remain in force

Option 2: Build a standing trade-remedy defence. Retain an international trade law firm in U.S. that will monitoring and fight cases, if proceedings are initiated under the regulation. Counsel cannot eliminate genuine subsidy exposure, but it can prevent Cambodia's rate from being determined unilaterally. 

  • Pros: Attacks the actual cause of the solar rate, which was the inability to litigate, not subsidy magnitude. Costs a fraction of one year’s duty exposure, especially on a critical sector.
  • Cons: Recurring budget and needs technical staff and most importantly, defends rather than prevents the issue.

Option 3: Audit subsidy exposure and diversify FDI sources. Map which Chinese owned exporters receive state support from China and monitor new inflows in exposed sectors.

  •  Pros: Identifies the exposure and limit external State subsidy before the U.S. decides to act. Diversification of FDI as part of risk management against a documented legal doctrine. 
  •  Cons: Sensitive with Beijing and with domestic trade interests. Non-Chinese capital has stayed away for reasons like transparency, dispute resolution, predictability and that take years to fix.

Recommendation

Adopt Options 2 now and initiate the process for Option 3, as Option 1 might leave us completely exposed to decision by U.S. & China. Solar was a warning, as the sectors U.S. exports were eliminated in eighteen months, not by policy action by Cambodia but by unilateral U.S. action. Cambodia cannot change who owns its factories within this planning cycle. It can decide whether the next determination of duties is contested, or conceded by default.
