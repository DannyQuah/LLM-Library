Milestone 1: Policy Memo: ZHU ELIZABETH ZHENGRAN

Managing ART Energy Commitment to Strengthen Indonesia’s Economic Security

To: Minister Airlangga Hartarto, Coordinating Minister for Economic Affairs

From: Policy Advisor, CMEA

Date: 18/09/2026

 

Summary

This memo assesses how Indonesia should implement the US energy purchase commitment under the US-Indonesia Agreement on Reciprocal Trade (ART). Fully meeting ART commitment could increase Indonesia’s exposure to energy supply concentration, while falling short could prompt US political pressure for concessions. Officials recommend progressively implementing the energy commitment while ART remains unratified, alongside stronger regional energy cooperation and faster delivery of existing energy-transition commitments.

 

Backgroud

On 19 February 2026, Indonesia and the US signed ART, reducing US “Liberation Day” tariffs on Indonesian exports from 32% to 19% and facilitating up to USD 33 billion in commercial deals. This includes Indonesia’s commitment to purchase USD 15 billion of US LPG, crude oil, and refined gasoline, which creates two potential economic security risks.

Risk 1: ART could shift energy supply from diversification to US concentration

Indonesia currently sources its oil and gas from across Southeast Asia, the Gulf, and Africa. Against this diversified portfolio, the USD 15 billion commitment would be equivalent to half of Indonesia’s 2025 oil and gas imports (USD 32.8 billion), or the combined value currently supplied by Singapore and Malaysia. The risk is the greatest for refined gasoline, Indonesia’s largest energy import (USD 21.3 billion in 2024) and the largest component of the energy commitment (USD 7 billion). The US currently supplies 0.08% of Indonesia’s gasoline, compared with 54% from Singapore and 21% from Malaysia. While adding the US would diversify the supplier base, the scale of the commitment could shift supply away from closer regional sources towards a more costly (USD 85 per barrel for US refined gasoline, compared with USD 80 for Singaporean supply) and distant supplier (i.e. longer shipping distances increase costs and disruption exposure).

This shift could create a broader economic security risk because energy shocks quickly transmit to the wider economy. Following a 50% rise in global oil prices after a Middle East supply shock in early 2026, Pertamax prices increased by 32% and demand for subsidized fuel surged in June. Higher fuel prices raise transport costs, increase pressure on subsidies, and add to the cost of living. Therefore, greater concentration on a distant supplier could leave Indonesia more exposed to energy shocks and less able to absorb their wider economic costs.

Risk 2: ART could reduce strategic flexibility while increasing US leverage

Meeting ART commitment could reduce Indonesia’s flexibility to diversify suppliers and negotiate better prices and terms as market conditions change. If total oil and gas imports do not increase, US purchases would largely displace Southeast Asian supplies, weakening regional supply networks and potentially straining ASEAN relations as the bloc seeks greater economic resilience.

ART commitment also creates political leverage for the US, despite not being a binding purchase quota. Indonesia is required to facilitate commercial arrangements, with actual purchases subject to commercial terms. However, the USD 15 billion target creates a political expectation by the US, so a shortfall could prompt pressure for higher purchases or concessions elsewhere in the bilateral relationship. This could constrain Indonesia’s ability to manage its energy diversification and broader relationship with the US on its own terms.

Window of opportunity: Pending ratification allows Indonesia to manage the trade-off

On 20 February 2026, one day after ART was signed, the US Supreme Court ruled that the “Liberation Day” tariffs were unlawful. ART has yet to be ratified or entered into force, as both governments are completing their domestic legal procedures. This creates a narrow window to shape the implementation of ART energy commitment. On 14 March 2026, the Government affirmed that ART remains the basis of Indonesia-US trade relations. Therefore, officials have assessed options for ratification that balance the risks of greater energy concentration against potential US pressure if Indonesia falls short of the commitment.

 

Option Analysis

Option 1: Renegotiate ART energy commitment before ratification

Indonesia could seek to reduce the USD 15 billion energy commitment or revise its composition and timing, using the US Supreme Court’s ruling to reopen ART before ratification. This would directly reduce Indonesia’s exposure to supply concentration risks.

However, the diplomatic costs may outweigh the benefits. Since the purchase commitment is not a legally binding quota and allows implementation flexibility, reopening the headline figure could expend diplomatic capital for limited gains. It could prompt further US demands on critical minerals or retaliation through alternative tariff measures under Sections 301 and 232. Therefore, reopening ART could further create friction with the US and put the broader benefits of ART at risk.

Option 2: Delay ratification to preserve strategic flexibility

Indonesia could delay ratification while awaiting clarity on the US position and completion of its domestic legal procedures. This would avoid near-term US energy purchases and associated costs, preserve existing supplier relationships and provide more time to assess US trade policy.

However, delay may provide only temporary flexibility. The US is seeking to rebuild tariff leverage through other statutory authorities and expects partners to implement their ART commitments. Therefore, prolonged delay could increase US pressure and undermine Indonesia’s credibility as a trade partner. More importantly, it would postpone rather than resolve the underlying trade-off, potentially leaving Indonesia with less bargaining room later.

Option 3: Implement unratified ART progressively while building energy resilience

Indonesia could maintain ART as the basis of bilateral trade relations while progressively increasing US energy purchases. This would demonstrate good-faith implementation without an abrupt shift towards US concentration. At the same time, existing measures to strengthen energy resilience can help mitigate these risks:

  • Strategic reserves: Indonesia is working to increase strategic oil reserves from around 20-25 days to 90 days and has proposed an ASEAN shared oil storage hub in Sumatra.
  • Oil demand reduction: Indonesia is pursuing electrification through USD 21.8 billion worth of Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP).
  • Supply diversification: Indonesia has expanded its oil suppliers to Russia and is deepening renewable energy cooperation with Germany and ASEAN partners.

However, these measures cannot fully offset near-term risks. Storage expansion remains constrained by capacity, ASEAN cooperation is uneven, and electrification will take time, with less than 20% of committed JETP funding utilized to date. Greater reliance on Russian oil could also expose Indonesia to additional US pressure following the expiry of the US waiver for Russian oil purchases in mid-2026.

 

Recommendation

Option 3 best balances the trade-off. It avoids the costs and uncertainty of reopening or delaying ART while providing a credible approach to managing the associated economic security risks. Therefore, it best protects Indonesia’s economic and strategic interests under current circumstances, while recognizing that the trade-off cannot be fully eliminated.

Implementation should focus on two priorities:

  1. Lead ASEAN efforts on regional energy resilience: Indonesia should use ongoing ASEAN discussions to develop the proposed regional oil stockpiling and sharing mechanism, including options for governance, financing, and stockholding responsibilities. This would turn Indonesia’s proposal into a concrete regional mechanism and strengthen its leadership in ASEAN cooperation.
  2. Accelerate delivery of existing energy-transition commitments: Indonesia should establish clear milestones for committed JETP projects, prioritizing those that reduce oil and gas dependence or strengthen near-term energy resilience. Officials should monitor implementation and address bottlenecks with development partners.
