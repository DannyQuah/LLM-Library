Milestone 1: Policy Memo: LIM JING XIANG, FELIX

To: H.E. Mr Songsak Thongsri, Deputy Prime Minister and Chairperson, Thai National Mekong Committee

From: Thai National Mekong Committee Secretariat

Date: 7 September 2026

Subject: Reducing Thailand’s Vulnerability to Upstream Dam Operations in the Mekong

 

Summary

Recent geopolitical tensions involving the weaponisation of natural resources to coerce state actions, most notably India’s continued abeyance of the Indus Waters Treaty with Pakistan in August 2026, have heightened concerns that Thailand could face similar vulnerabilities arising from its dependence on upstream Mekong states (i.e. Myanmar, China) to regulate upstream dam operations. Thailand should seek to close both legal and economical gaps that leave it exposed to accidental disruption or future potential economic leverage by upstream states.

Thailand can prioritise commercial engagement to secure upstream operating safeguards discreetly from upstream states, while starting to develop independent monitoring and community warning systems. Together, these measures will make cooperation more attractive to upstream partners and help protect Thai communities when information is delayed or cooperation fails.

 

Background

Upstream dams in have already changed the Mekong's physical behaviour. In 2022, the Mekong River Commission (MRC) detected daily water-level fluctuations downstream of the Xayaburi project in Laos that could affect ecosystems, alongside declining sediment loads. A 2025 study also found significant dam-related changes in dry-season flows reported by communities in Chiang Rai and Loei. While these findings do not point to deliberate coercive action, it establishes the potential impact upstream states can inflict through manipulation of Mekong River flows to downstream Thai communities.

In addition, the current established governance system in MRC maintains full operational discretion to upstream states. Myanmar, which operates upstream dams, is an MRC member, but is not legally obliged to halt dam operations if Thailand raises objections. China is an MRC Dialogue Partner and is not bound by MRC guidelines. Although China has shared hydrological data from its two Upper Mekong stations, these are cooperative arrangements rather than a binding requirement to consult Thailand over dam operating decisions.

This risk has become more salient because strategic resources are increasingly drawn into unrelated disputes. In April 2025, India linked continued operation of the Indus Waters Treaty to Pakistan's conduct on cross-border terrorism. An arbitral court ruled in August 2026 that it remained in force, but India has rejected the ruling. Separately, China's 2025 rare-earth controls became a leverage tool in reciprocal trade bargaining with the United States. Both incidents show that resource interdependence can become bargaining leverage during a crisis.

For information, Thailand has commercial and power-generation interests in immediate upstream dams in Laos. Thailand is the principal buyer and investor of some of Laos future hydropower plants which gives us potential commercial leverage. In 2025, the Electricity Generating Authority of Thailand (EGAT) contracted a significant 5,934.9 MW from Laos. Thailand can potentially employ the same commercial strategy for further upstream dam projects to expand our influence on upstream dam operations.

 

Analysis and Options

Thailand must take active steps now to minimize the dam-operations gap with further upstream neighbours before it can be used to as leverage against our interests in the future. It is crucial, however, that Thailand pursue this objective discreetly, as publicly framing upstream dams as potential instruments of coercion can provoke defensiveness and complicate future cooperation with Myanmar and China. Potential options are outlined below.

 

Option 1: Link commercial benefits to Thai operational control of upstream dams

For Myanmar, Thailand can invest in and build future hydropower dams through its private companies as we did with Laos (see Background). Afterwhich, we can negotiate joint operating provisions tied to electricity-purchase agreements as well. In exchange for joint operational control, EGAT can offer predictable purchasing schedules or compensate Myanmar and relevant investors for additional costs of flow adjustments from Thai requests. This approach makes cooperation commercially attractive and worthwhile, and targets Myanmar’s dam operations though incentives that Thailand can potentially influence.

For China, its dams are usually funded and controlled by Chinese state government, which Thailand is likely to have minimal commercial influence upon. Instead, Thailand can encourage Chinese businesses to form joint ventures with Thai community enterprises in river-dependent activities such as fishing operations and river tourism. Chinese capital and profits will share some exposure to the consequences of upstream flow disruption by upstream dams. This can potentially give Chinese investors an incentive to advocate for predictable and controlled dam operations to Chinese authorities, aligning the Chinese interests to ours.

However, directly inviting Chinese businesses will face restrictions under Thailand’s Foreign Business Act, making Chinese investors investing in local fisheries or tourist vendors a more practical starting point. These projects will be regulated by Thai authorities through complementary new legislation to protect existing local fishing and land rights. This ensures partnerships continuously provide meaningful community participation and income to downstream Thai communities as well.

 

Option 2: Work with MRC members for legally binding control and invite China to be a member

Thailand can initiate confidential discussions with downstream MRC members (e.g., Cambodia, Vietnam) to build support for delegating decision-making powers over all dam operations to the Commission instead of relying on individual state decisions. Their shared exposure to upstream flow disruptions provides common interest in protecting downstream livelihoods and economic activity. Once consensus is reached, we can propose the idea as a lower-Mekong bloc to MRC. Concurrently, Thailand (and MRC) can invite China to join MRC as a full member, including explicit acceptance of the revised operational rules for its upstream dams. This will bring Chinese dams within an agreed legal framework that Thailand can have meaningful influence over.

However, any amendments to the 1995 Mekong Agreement require all parties’ agreement which may not be received favourably by upstream states. Moreover, Thailand may have to accept reciprocal obligations by upstream states in exchange for restrictions on sovereign operating discretion.

 

Option 3: Maintain status quo and prioritise internal monitoring and community initiatives

Thailand can strengthen its own independent river gauges and forecasting systems, supplement them with satellite observations, and connect alerts directly to exposed communities and water-service operators discreetly. Investments can also include adjustable water intakes, emergency water storage and locally developed response plans. These measures will build on the existing MRC monitoring network by supplementing Thailand’s own monitoring and mitigation capabilities. This option is largely within Thailand’s control and remains useful when upstream cooperation fails, potentially reducing any downstream damage through early warning and evacuation.

However, implementing this requires sustained public funding and cannot prevent harmful releases or ecological disruption. Satellite observations can supplement monitoring, but they cannot influence operating decision that upstream states make.

 

Recommendation

The Secretariat recommends prioritising Option 1, complemented by Option 3. Option 1 provides the strongest initial approach as it offers Myanmar more tangible incentives for compliance while preserving discreet engagement. EGAT can identify potential future Myanmar projects and prepare a proposal linking commercial benefits to Thailand’s access to Myanmar’s dam control. The Chinese partnerships can be developed progressively later, recognising that influence on Chinese dam operations remains unlikely.

Concurrently, Thailand can start developing Option 3 by strengthening river monitoring and connecting warnings directly to vulnerable communities and water-service operators. These measures will provide early warning, verify upstream information and support early action if notifications are delayed or cooperation fails.

 

For your concurrence, please. Thank you.
