Milestone 1: Policy Memo: BAMARAMANNAGE DON ANGELICA SHENALI

MEMORANDUM

To: Dr Kan Zaw, Union Minister for Finance and Revenue
From: Shenali Bamaramannage, Policy Analyst
Date: 13.09.2026
Subject: Funding human-capital development in Shan State through rare-earth revenues and strategic partnerships

SUMMARY

Rare-earth mining is expanding across Shan State as Myanmar’s heavy rare earths have become important to China and global supply chains. Yet the extraction model undermines the productive base on which the industry depends. Rare-earth mining relies on local communities, but environmental degradation and unsafe working conditions erode their health and productive capacity. An industry cannot sustain itself while weakening the human capital that powers it.

Myanmar should therefore establish a Shan Community Advancement from Rare-earth Extraction (CARE) Fund, financed by mandatory contributions from mining operators and a share of existing government revenues. Revenues should be reinvested in community health and skill development. Earmarking public revenues would demonstrate shared responsibility and make mandatory operator contributions more acceptable. Meanwhile, cultivating alternative partnerships with other countries would strengthen Myanmar’s bargaining position to negotiate for more funding and better conditions. It may also help secure complementary expertise and investment from new foreign partners.

BACKGROUND

Myanmar’s rare-earth resources generate considerable economic value but little corresponding investment in workers. China is the largest buyer, with Myanmar having exported more than USD 3.6 billion in rare earths to China between 2021 and 2024. This dependence runs both ways. Myanmar has supplied roughly two-thirds of China’s rare-earth imports by volume in recent years, including heavy rare earths such as dysprosium and terbium, which are critical inputs for dual-use items like electric vehicles and heat-resistant magnets.

The immediate policy challenge is to ensure that this strategically important sector does not weaken its own productive foundations. Kachin State demonstrates the risks of expanding extraction without corresponding investment in producing communities. Nearly 400 mining sites now operate across Kachin, almost three times the number before 2021. This has polluted water and soil, damaged agriculture, and exposed workers and communities to serious health risks. Shan offers an opportunity to avoid reproducing this model. Nineteen mines were identified around Mong Yawng in 2025. Environmental costs are already appearing. Farmland has been damaged, while contamination in rivers entering Thailand has been linked to upstream mining in Shan.

These effects directly affect the sector’s performance. Poor worker health and weak skills reduce productivity and increase worker turnover. In this way, extraction without corresponding investment risks eroding the human-capital base that sustains production.

Myanmar’s growing importance as a mineral supplier creates an opportunity to address this underinvestment by seeking better terms for mining communities. India has begun discussing rare-earth cooperation with Myanmar, creating a potential alternative source of investment and demand. This could reduce the constraints imposed by reliance on a single dominant buyer.

OPTIONS

Option 1: Establish the Shan Community Advancement from Rare-earth Extraction (CARE) Fund

Myanmar could channel part of the value generated by extraction back into mining communities. The fund could be financed through two closely related mechanisms. These mechanisms are compatible and could be used together, but differ in who bears the cost.

Approach 1A: Earmark a share of government revenues

Myanmar could earmark a fixed share of existing taxes and other revenues for the fund. Unlike the current system, in which most extractive revenues enter broader government finances without a link to producing communities, this would create a connection between mineral production in Shan and investment in the health and skills of affected communities. Similar revenue-sharing arrangements operate elsewhere. Peru, for example, transfers 50% of mining-company income-tax revenues to regional and local governments in producing areas.

The objective is to ensure that communities supporting a nationally valuable industry receive a portion of its fiscal benefits. However, the fund would compete with other government priorities for existing mineral revenues, while receipts could fluctuate with mineral prices and production. Earmarking also does not require operators themselves to bear a direct portion of the health and environmental costs associated with their activities.

Approach 1B: Require mandatory operator contributions

Myanmar could additionally require mining operators to contribute directly to the fund according to a standardized formula. The Philippines provides a precedent, requiring mining operators to allocate at least 1.5% of annual operating costs to social-development and related programs, including health services and education.

This mechanism explicitly makes investment in the communities supporting extraction part of the cost of doing business. It also creates a dedicated funding stream without requiring government to divert as much existing revenue from other priorities. However, additional mandatory contributions raise operators’ costs and, if set too high, could discourage investment or encourage avoidance. Required contributions may also be ineffective without mechanisms to verify what companies owe and actually pay. Imposing outsized obligations on Chinese-linked operators could create commercial and diplomatic friction, especially while Myanmar has few credible alternative investors.

Option 2: Use buyer diversification as leverage

Myanmar could retain China as its principal market while cultivating India and others as complementary investors. Among potential alternatives, India is likely the most viable investor after China. India and Myanmar discussed rare-earth cooperation and mining investment in June 2026, reflecting India’s push for greater supply-chain flexibility and expanding engagement. Yet even India is no substitute for China given its processing advantages. More generally, diverting investment or exports toward newer alternatives could mean higher costs and potentially lower returns in the short run—an economic price Myanmar would pay for reducing dependence on a single buyer.

Importantly, China’s dependence on Myanmar’s heavy rare earths gives Myanmar bargaining leverage despite its own reliance on Chinese investment and processing. Cultivating credible alternative partners would strengthen this position further. Myanmar could use interest from India and the rest of the world to press for worker training and occupational-health provisions in mineral-access agreements.

RECOMMENDATION

Myanmar should establish the CARE Fund using both mechanisms under Option 1, while pursuing Option 2 for diversified foreign investment. Option 1 can be implemented relatively quickly with greater domestic control, while Option 2 will require sustained diplomacy but could reinforce Option 1B over time.

First, require operators to make a modest standardized contribution to the fund and supplement this with a defined share of tax revenue. Combining the mechanisms is preferable because it shares the cost between government and industry, creating a more stable funding base. Option 1A signals that responsibility for sustaining mining communities is shared, making company contributions under Option 1B more politically acceptable. Under either approach, the fund should prioritize occupational health and skill development. An independent governing body comprising government, affected-community, and industry representatives should oversee allocations. Contributions and expenditures should be published and independently audited, with mining companies represented in governance but not controlling distributions.

The Ministry of Finance and Revenue should coordinate with the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to pursue limited investment and offtake agreements with India while managing relations with China. Finance should define the investment and CARE Fund terms sought from foreign partners, while Foreign Affairs identifies credible partners and incorporates these objectives into economic diplomacy. This would strengthen Myanmar’s position in future negotiations with Chinese operators, where CARE Fund contributions and worker and environmental protections should become standard conditions of mineral access overtime. Moreover, negotiations with India and others could provide complementary expertise and investment in improving living conditions for mining communities.
