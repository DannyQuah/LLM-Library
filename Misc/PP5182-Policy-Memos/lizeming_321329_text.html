Milestone 1: Policy Memo: LI ZEMING

To: Chair, National Electric Vehicle Policy Committee

From: LI ZEMING

Date: 17 Sep 2026

Subject: Reducing Supply Chain Concentration Risk in Thailand’s EV Manufacturing Base

 

Summary

 

Thailand’s EV strategy has reduced dependence on imported vehicles but created a new concentration risk in China-led domestic production. Chinese manufacturers now dominate Thailand’s mass-market battery-electric vehicle industry, while Thai firms retain limited control over battery cells, power electronics, vehicle platforms and supplier selection. The immediate risk is not Chinese investment itself, but Thailand’s inability to sustain EV production if China-linked parent companies or suppliers experience disruption.

 

The EV Board should introduce a post-EV3.5 Supply Chain Resilience Programme. Thailand should remain open to Chinese investment while making enhanced incentives conditional on high-value domestic production, supply-chain transparency, Thai supplier development and qualified alternative sourcing. This approach would reduce concentration risk while limiting the cost increases associated with forced decoupling.

 

Background: Why This Is a Priority Now

 

Thailand’s EV policy has successfully accelerated both adoption and investment. In 2025, battery-electric vehicles accounted for 19.6% of new vehicle registrations.1 By July 2026, the Board of Investment had approved 18 Battery Electric Vehicle (BEV) projects with planned annual production capacity exceeding 370,000 vehicles. However, mass-market production is concentrated among Chinese-owned manufacturers, including BYD, SAIC-MG, Great Wall Motor, GAC Aion, Changan and Chery. Non-Chinese BEV production by Mercedes-Benz, BMW and Hyundai remains comparatively limited or recent.

 

Local assembly has therefore not eliminated external dependence. Vehicles imported from China accounted for approximately three-quarters of Thailand’s electric-car market in 2025.2 At the same time, most new mass-market BEV capacity in Thailand is owned by Chinese manufacturers. Moreover, Thailand still lacks widespread domestic production of high-voltage batteries, high-voltage systems and external charging connectors.3 Thai factories may assemble vehicles from imported cells and components but vehicle platforms, software,  supplier selection and key technologies remain controlled abroad.

 

This concentration creates risks. A disruption affecting one country or a small number of parent companies could interrupt Thai production. Limited domestic capabilities reduce the share of EV value retained by Thai workers and suppliers. Finally, dependence on foreign-controlled platforms weakens Thailand’s bargaining power over future investment, technology transfer and sourcing decisions.

 

Action is urgent because the present incentive cycle is approaching its end. EV3.5 runs through 2027,4 while the temporary treatment of imported battery cells under local-content rules was scheduled to expire in June 2026.5 The design of the post-EV3.5 regime will determine whether Thailand develops a diversified, higher-value EV ecosystem or locks itself into foreign-controlled assembly.

 

Analysis and Policy Options

Option 1: Directly Restrict Chinese Vehicles and Components

Thailand could use higher tariffs, import quotas, country-specific sourcing limits or incentives reserved for non-Chinese manufacturers.

 

Pros:

  • Could accelerate diversification of brands and imports.
  • Creates additional market space for Japanese, Korean, European and domestic firms.

Cons:

  • Would probably increase EV prices and slow consumer adoption.
  • Alternative suppliers cannot yet replace Chinese battery and component capacity at comparable scale and cost.
  • Could discourage existing investors, provoke commercial retaliation and weaken Thai exports to China.
  • Country-specific restrictions and mandatory local-content conditions could raise concerns under WTO non-discrimination and trade-related investment rules.6

 

Option 2: Introduce an Open but Conditional Resilience Strategy

Thailand could remain open to manufacturers from all countries while redesigning post-EV3.5 incentives around capabilities and resilience. Additional support would be linked to activities performed in Thailand—such as engineering, testing, worker training, battery management, power electronics and recycling—rather than the nationality of the investor.

 

Manufacturers receiving enhanced support would also disclose critical supply dependencies, maintain business-continuity plans and qualify alternative suppliers. In parallel, BOI would target additional investment from Korea, Japan, Europe, India and ASEAN.

 

Pros:

  • Reduces concentration risk without forcing Chinese investors to leave.
  • Encourages Thai participation in higher-value production and technology.
  • Preserves competition and relatively affordable EV prices.


Cons:

  • Diversification would take several years rather than occurring immediately.
  • Supply-chain reporting and verification would increase administrative costs.

Option 3: Build an ASEAN EV Supply Chain Compact

Rather than attempting to localize the entire EV supply chain within Thailand, the government could pursue a regional production network with selected ASEAN partners.  The compact could build on the 2023 ASEAN Leaders’Declaration on Developing Regional Electric Vehicle Ecosystem.7 Thailand would remain the principal vehicle-production and testing hub, while sourcing complementary capabilities from countries such as Indonesia for battery minerals and processed materials, Malaysia for semiconductors and power electronics, and Vietnam for electrical and manufactured components.

 

The compact could include accelerated customs procedures, common battery and charging standards, mutual recognition of product testing, coordinated investment promotion and shared monitoring of critical supply risks.

 

Pros:

  • Uses regional specializationto achieve diversification at lower cost than national self-sufficiency.
  • Gives manufacturers access to a larger regional market and greater economies of scale.
  • Strengthens Thailand’s position as the coordinating hub of an ASEAN EV production network.
  • Reduces reliance on direct imports from China without explicitly discriminating against Chinese companies.
  • Makes ASEAN more attractive to Japanese, Korean, European and Indian investors.

Cons:

  • Requires difficult coordination among ASEAN governments with competing industrial ambitions.
  • Indonesia, Malaysia and Vietnam may seek to replace rather than complement Thailand as the regional EV hub.
  • Regional suppliers may themselves remain dependent on Chinese capital, technology or intermediate inputs, producing only superficial diversification.8

 

Recommendation

 

Thailand should adopt Option 2, supported by regional cooperation under Option 3. The objective should be to maintain Chinese investment while reducing the likelihood and duration of production disruptions.

 

First, by March 2027, BOI, Customs and the Thailand Automotive Institute should establish an EV Supply Chain Resilience Unit. Incentive recipients should disclose the production location and ownership of suppliers for battery cells, processed materials, semiconductors, motors, inverters and battery-management systems. The unit should publish an annual aggregate concentration dashboard.

 

Second, the post-EV3.5 regime should introduce a Resilience Incentive Score from 2028. Enhanced support should reward research, engineering, technical training, component production, testing, repair and recycling activities performed in Thailand. Incentives should support domestic capabilities rather than require firms to purchase a fixed proportion of Thai goods, reducing potential conflict with WTO local-content rules.

 

Third, recipients of enhanced incentives should demonstrate “two-source readiness” for designated critical components. Firms should qualify backup suppliers in separate production locations and participate in annual disruption exercises, without being required to meet government purchasing quotas. Backup suppliers should be assessed for shared ownership, upstream dependencies and exposure to the same disruption.

 

Fourth, the government should establish a three-year supplier transition facility offering matching grants, testing support and loan guarantees. It should prioritize battery management, power electronics, motors, thermal systems and recycling. World Bank analysis identifies approximately 250 Thai suppliers capable of adapting existing products and another 67 with potential to enter more advanced EV activities.3

 

Finally, Thailand should pursue an ASEAN resilience compact covering common standards, mutual recognition of testing, faster customs procedures and a regional supplier database.

 

By 2027, BOI should establish baseline dependencies and propose a costed pilot budget. Annual reviews should track reporting coverage, the availability and switching time of alternative suppliers, and Thai suppliers’ entry into higher-value EV contracts.
